# Content Strategy — Market Validation

Tested against published research, the Indian creator landscape, platform data
and advertising regulation. 29 August 2026.

---

## 1. EVERY HEALTH CLAIM CHECKED OUT — most are understated

| Claim in the strategy | Reality | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "Low Vitamin D is extremely common in India" | Metropolis analysis of **2.2 million tests** (2019–Jan 2025): **46.5% deficient, 26% insufficient = ~72% inadequate.** South India 51.6%. Children/adolescents 66.4% | ✅ Understated |
| "Haemoglobin below 12 in women: common, not normal" | NFHS-5: **57% of women 15–49 anaemic**, up from 53% in NFHS-4. Children 67.1% | ✅ Verified, and **worsening** |
| "Indians get diabetes at lower BMI" / sugar content | ICMR-INDIAB (Lancet, 2023): **101M diabetic, 136M prediabetic.** Hypertension 35.5% | ✅ Verified |
| "SGPT raised and you don't drink? Fatty liver" | Meta-analysis of 50 studies: **~1 in 3 Indian adults** has NAFLD (38.6%); 52.8% in high-risk groups | ✅ Verified |
| Cholesterol content (pillar 2) | ICMR-INDIAB: **dyslipidaemia 81.2%** — 213M with high cholesterol; **abdominal obesity 39.5% (351M people)** | ✅ Massively understated |

**The single most useful number found:** in the ICMR-INDIAB Jammu data, **40% of people with diabetes were undiagnosed.** That is the entire "get tested" thesis in one statistic.

### Two corrections to make before posting
1. **Vitamin D is improving, not worsening** — deficiency fell from ~51% (2019–20) to ~43% (2023–24). Don't frame it as a growing crisis; frame it as still-huge.
2. **Anaemia genuinely is worsening** (53% → 57%), despite Anaemia Mukt Bharat. That one *can* carry urgency, and it's the stronger post of the two.

**Content implication:** the topic bank is aimed at the largest health problems in the country — 81% dyslipidaemia, 72% inadequate Vitamin D, 57% of women anaemic, a third with fatty liver, 40% of diabetes undiagnosed. Nothing in the bank is a niche concern.

## 2. THE COMPETITIVE PICTURE — better than expected, for one specific reason

### The doctor-creator space is crowded and growing fast
**~12,400 Indian medical professionals** created content in 2025 — **47,900 posts, 206M engagements.** Competing on medical authority would be a losing game.

### But doctors are legally barred from the exact content this strategy is built on
The NMC Professional Conduct Regulations (Aug 2023) prohibit registered doctors from:
- Posting **patient scans, reports or images**
- **Patient testimonials** with the doctor featured
- Self-promotion, soliciting patients, endorsing products
- Discussing treatment or prescribing on public social media

**This is a structural gap, not a marketing angle.** A licensed doctor *cannot* build "here is a real report, line by line" as a content format. A non-doctor working with consented, anonymised reports can. The "I'm not a doctor" position isn't a weakness to be apologised for — it's the only reason this content format is available at all.

### Product competitors exist and are further along than assumed
Already live in India: **Bharat Swasth, Seevo, ReportSense, testresult.ai, aidiagme.** Seevo covers English/Hindi/Malayalam/Kannada. **Bharat Swasth explicitly markets ICMR-aligned Indian reference ranges across 60+ Indian labs** rather than Western ranges.

Two consequences:
1. **The content lane is far emptier than the product lane.** Searching for Indian creators doing report-decoding on Instagram returned websites and YouTube — not Instagram accounts. Content is the differentiated move; the app alone is not.
2. **This is a product warning, not just a content one.** A competitor has made *Indian reference ranges* their headline claim, while our 66 ranges are adult-generic and not clinically reviewed — the exact weakness already flagged in `DATA-LAYER-BRIEF.md` §4. That gap is now a competitive one.

## 3. ⚠️ THE COMPLIANCE GAP — the strategy did not account for this

**ASCI Influencer Guidelines, Addendum II (7 April 2025)** — this binds Rohit, unlike NMC which only binds doctors.

- Health/nutrition influencers must hold **relevant qualifications** (medical, nutrition, dietetics, etc.) **for the kind of advice they give**
- Those **without** qualifications may share **only generic information — not technical advice**, or public-service messages
- Qualification/disclaimer must appear **upfront** — superimposed at the start of a video or as the opening remark, **not** at the end
- The Consumer Affairs Ministry has parallel rules requiring anyone presenting as a health expert to carry clear disclaimers; breaches can attract penalties under the **Consumer Protection Act 2019**

**Why this reaches us:** ASCI's remit is advertising. A page with the app in the bio is brand content, which pulls it into scope.

### What this changes in practice

| Risk | Fix |
|---|---|
| Disclaimer sits at the **end** of every script | Move it to the **first frame** — on-screen at 0:00 and the opening line of the caption |
| Content states what a value **means for you** | Reframe to what the report **says** and **what to ask your doctor** — factual and verifiable, not advisory |
| No stated qualification | State the opposite, upfront and permanently: **"Not a doctor. Health information, not medical advice."** Honest disclosure *is* the compliance posture |
| Nutrition pillar is the most exposed | Keep to habits, timing, portions and swaps. No dosages, no "cures", no supplement brand names |
| No medical review | **Get a doctor to review content before it posts** |

### The doctor-reviewer recommendation

One qualified reviewer solves **three separate problems at once**:
1. Brings the content inside ASCI's qualification expectation
2. Clinically validates the 66 reference ranges — already flagged as needed in the data brief
3. Adds real credibility to both the page and the app

A "Medically reviewed by Dr. X" line is worth more than any hook in the bank. This was previously a nice-to-have; the research makes it the highest-value unblocked action available.

## 4. AUDIENCE — one assumption was wrong

| Assumption | Data | Action |
|---|---|---|
| "The caregiver is usually the daughter" | Caregiving research supports women as primary caregivers — **but Instagram India is 67% male / 33% female** | Write the family health manager as **the son at least as often as the daughter.** The line "usually the daughter" is true in life and mismatched to the platform |
| Lead segment 28–45 | 25–34 is the largest Reels cohort (33.3%); most Indian users are 18–34 | Sound, but skew the lower end — 28–38 is the sweet spot |
| Caregiver niche is underserved | UNFPA: elderly population doubling to **347M by 2050**, outnumbering children by 2046. India-specific caregiving-burden data is **thin** — mostly US/China studies | Under-researched = under-served. The demographic wave is real and the content space is genuinely open |

**Scale check:** India has ~400–480M Instagram users and leads global Reels consumption. Reach is not the constraint. Trust is.

## 5. WHAT THE RESEARCH DIDN'T SUPPORT

- **No evidence of an existing Indian anti-restriction food movement** with a clear leader. The "boiled food is not health food" flag looks genuinely unclaimed — good news, but it also means it's unproven. Treat post #2 as the real experiment of week 1.
- **Health misinformation is a live, documented problem** (First Check/DataLEADS: diabetes, obesity and lifestyle disease misinformation is the most prevalent category). Myth-busting has demand — and one factual error on a health page attached to a health product costs far more than it would elsewhere.

## 6. REVISED SCORE

**8.5 / 10** — up from 8. The research strengthened the thesis more than it damaged it.

**What went up:**
- Every health claim verified, several understated
- The "not a doctor" position turned out to be a *structural* advantage doctors are legally barred from copying
- The content lane is emptier than the product lane — content is genuinely the right move
- Topic selection maps onto the country's largest health burdens

**What's still missing the last 1.5:**
- **Compliance was absent from the plan** (−1). Fixable this week, but it was a real hole.
- **No distribution plan** (−0.5). Still "post and hope". No collaborations, no seeding, no comment strategy on larger accounts.

**To reach 9.5:** add the doctor reviewer, move disclaimers upfront, and write down one distribution mechanism beyond posting.
